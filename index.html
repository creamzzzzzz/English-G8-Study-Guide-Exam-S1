What is on the exam

You have explored how language, both persuasive and poetic, can influence thinking and share experiences and beliefs. The exam asks you to show that skill on a poem you have never seen.

The taskOne extended paragraph
The textAn unseen poem
The length14 to 20 sentences
The questionHow poetic techniques share an experience or belief

Your paragraph must include

A clear theme statementThe big idea the poem communicates about its topic.
The poet’s intentionsWhat the poet wants to say and why.
The impact on an audienceWhat the reader thinks or feels as a result.
Quotations as evidenceIntegrated correctly into your own sentences.
Language and/or structureYour choice of which to analyse, or both.
The word that matters

How means you analyse the method. Name the technique, point to it in a quotation, then explain what it makes the audience think or feel.

The three assessed criteria

One paragraph is marked on all three at once. Open a strand to see what it asks for.

Criterion A

Analysing

A2Identify and explain the effects of the creator’s choices on an audience.

In plain words: name a technique, then say what it makes the reader think or feel.

Show it by: technique, then quotation, then “As a result, the reader…”.

A3Justify opinions and ideas, using examples, explanations and terminology.

In plain words: every claim about the poem needs proof and reasoning.

Show it by: a quotation as the example, an explanation of the key word, and exact terms such as personification.

Criterion B

Organising

B1Employ organisational structures that serve the context and intention.

In plain words: use the shape of a formal analytical paragraph.

Show it by: topic sentence, developing sentences, supporting sentences, concluding sentence.

B2Organise opinions and ideas in a sustained, coherent and logical manner.

In plain words: your ideas build step by step and keep returning to the theme.

Show it by: linking words such as as a result and overall, and ending each point on the theme.

B3Integrate quotations correctly.

In plain words: quotations sit inside your own sentences, exact, short and cited.

Show it by: lead-in words, then “quotation”, then (Poet line).

Criterion D

Using Language

D1Use appropriate and varied vocabulary, sentence structures and forms of expression.

In plain words: avoid repeating “shows”, and mix longer and shorter sentences.

Show it by: the verb bank and sentence patterns in the Language section.

D2Write in a register and style that serve the context and intention.

In plain words: your audience is academic and formal.

Show it by: third person, present tense, precise terminology, no slang.

D3Use correct grammar, syntax and punctuation.

In plain words: sentences are complete, controlled and punctuated accurately.

Show it by: the proofreading passes.

D4Spell with accuracy.

In plain words: including every technique name.

Show it by: the terminology spelling bank.

The frame

Opening (1 to 2 sentences)

Optional hook, then the poem title, the poet, your theme statement and the techniques you will analyse.

Closing (1 to 2 sentences)

Restate how the techniques present the idea, then state the message or impact on the audience.

Quotations as evidence

A quotation is proof, so it must be exact, short and woven into your sentence. Then you analyse it.

Three levels of integration

Dropped in

Frost writes about gold. “Nature’s first green is gold” (Frost 1).

The quotation floats alone and is not part of a sentence. Avoid.

Introduced

Frost introduces this idea through the metaphor “Nature’s first green is gold” (Frost 1).

A lead-in names the technique and carries the quotation. Secure.

Woven in

By calling spring’s first leaves “gold” (Frost 1), the poet presents them as treasure.

Quotation rules

Copy exactlyKeep the poet’s spelling, wording and capitals.
Keep it shortA phrase or one word is often enough to analyse.
Use quotation marksEvery borrowed word sits inside them, and poem titles do too.
Cite the linePoet’s surname and line number, as in (Frost 1).
Lead in, then analyseNever leave a quotation without explaining its effect.
Vary your lead-insAvoid repeating “the quote shows” in every sentence.
Criterion A · Analysing

Model paragraph, colour-coded

A worked example on Robert Frost’s “Nothing Gold Can Stay”: 17 sentences. Tap a colour to light up only that job, and watch the same cycle repeat.

Every sentence has a job. Choose a colour above to see that job on its own.

1Even the most beautiful moments eventually give way to something ordinary, often before anyone has even noticed. 2“Nothing Gold Can Stay” by Robert Frost suggests that change is quiet and unavoidable, and that what is most precious can easily be replaced by something plain and ordinary without any dramatic warning through the use of metaphor, personification, and imagery. 3Frost introduces this idea through the metaphor “Nature’s first green is gold” (Frost 1). 4This compares the delicate first leaves of spring to a precious metal, and the word “gold” captures both their shimmering colour and the value society places on gold as a precious metal. 5As a result, the reader pictures the beginning of the season as a treasure, which creates a feeling of wonder before any loss is mentioned. 6This establishes what is at stake in the theme, because the more precious the beginning appears, the more painful its disappearance becomes. 7It also suggests that people only recognise the value of a moment once it is almost over. 8Frost then goes on to personify nature as a figure who struggles to protect this treasure, stating that gold is “Her hardest hue to hold” (Frost 2). 9By giving nature human qualities, the poet turns a changing season into a struggle, and the word “hue” gives nature the eye of a painter who watches a carefully created colour begin to fade. 10This makes the reader sympathise with nature, while the verb “hold” creates a sense of effort and helplessness, as though something is slipping through the speaker’s fingers. 11This reflects how people try to hold on to what they love, but holding on to a moment is as impossible as holding on to a colour, which strengthens the theme that change is unavoidable. 12The poem’s turning point arrives in the image “Then leaf subsides to leaf” (Frost 5). 13The repetition of the word “leaf” shows the golden stage of nature returning to something ordinary, and the use of the word “subsides,” usually used for a wave or a feeling gently disappearing or going away, suggests a change so gradual that it is barely noticed by the person observing. 14The flatness of the line surprises the reader, who may expect a dramatic ending but instead experiences a calm fading, which makes the loss feel quieter and more realistic. 15This develops the theme by suggesting that change is not always a disaster but a settling into the ordinary, so the real sadness lies in the loss of wonder rather than in any sudden catastrophe. 16Overall, Frost uses metaphor, personification, and imagery to present beautiful things as precious because they are brief. 17The poem clearly shows that individuals should notice and appreciate good moments in their lives before, like the gold in the poem, those treasures quietly disappear.

What to notice · A

Each technique is named, tied to a quotation, and its effect on the reader is explained. Every point finishes by strengthening the theme.

What to notice · B

The same cycle repeats three times between a framing opening and closing. Each quotation arrives inside a sentence, with a citation.

What to notice · D

Formal, present tense and third person. The verbs vary: captures, establishes, reflects, strengthens, suggests.

Criterion D · Using Language

Academic tone and varied language

Your audience is academic and formal. Use precise words, write in the present tense, and keep the poet and the poem as your subject.

Criteria D3 and D4

Proofreading strategies

Read the paragraph several times, each time looking for one kind of problem.

Pass 1Meaning and logicDoes every sentence do a job and link to the theme?
Pass 2Grammar and punctuationComplete sentences, commas, apostrophes, doubled marks.
Pass 3Spelling and terminologyEvery technique name spelled correctly.
Pass 4QuotationsExact wording, quotation marks and a citation each time.

Common slips

Tense

Write about the poem in the present tense.

Frost used metaphor → Frost uses metaphor

Doubled punctuation

Check the end of lists and clauses for stray marks.

metaphor, imagery,. → metaphor and imagery.

Spelling style

Choose British or American spelling and keep it throughout.

colour and sympathize → colour and sympathise

Agreement

The subject and verb must match.

Frost use → Frost uses

Poem titles

Put titles in quotation marks, with capitals on the main words.

“Nothing Gold Can Stay”

Tone

Remove contractions, “you”, slang and “I think”.

it doesn’t change → it does not change
